## POST 3: The Difference Between a Customer Who Buys Less and One Who's About to Leave Forever

**Hook Line:**
Your business probably thinks these are the same problem. They're not. And confusing them is costing you serious money.

**Body:**
I need to make a distinction that most businesses never make.

There's a difference between a customer whose purchase frequency is declining and a customer who is entering a churn pattern.

Sounds like the same thing. It's absolutely not.

**A customer whose frequency is declining** might be:
- Seasonal in their buying behavior
- Going through a slow period but will be back
- Consolidating their orders (buying bigger, less often)
- Trying a competitor but hasn't abandoned you
- Experiencing their own business cycle and it's not about you

**A customer entering a churn pattern** is:
- Shifting from your product/service to a competitor
- Dissatisfied and actively looking to leave
- Moving away from your market entirely
- Going out of business
- No longer needs what you sell

Here's why the distinction matters: your response is completely different.

For a customer whose frequency is declining, your strategy is to understand the cause and address it. Maybe they need a different product. Maybe they need a volume discount. Maybe they're in a low season and you should check back in Q3. You don't panic, you adapt.

For a customer entering a churn pattern, your strategy is urgent retention. Personal call. Exit interview. VIP offer. Whatever it takes. Because the pattern is leading somewhere.

Here's how RFM helps you distinguish:

If recency is stable but frequency is dropping slowly over six months, that's declining behavior. Monitor, don't panic.

If recency is growing (longer between purchases) AND frequency is dropping AND monetary value is declining all at the same time, that's a churn pattern. Intervene now.

One company I worked with didn't make this distinction. They had a customer who went from $2,000/month to $1,500/month. They assumed it was seasonal and did nothing. Six months later, that customer was gone entirely. They never intervened because they misidentified the problem.

But when they started looking for true churn patterns (the combination of all three RFM metrics declining), they caught 23 customers in month 2-3 of the pattern. They saved 17 of them with targeted retention. Revenue save: $1.8M annually.

**Closing Punch Line:**
Data quality without pattern recognition is just noise. Knowing the difference between decline and churn is the difference between good retention and great retention.

**CTA:**

## POST 7: The Data Question Every Business with 1,000+ Customers Should Be Asking

**Hook Line:**
If you have 1,000+ customers and you haven't asked this question yet, you're leaving money on the table.

**Body:**
The question is: "What percentage of my revenue comes from what percentage of my customers?"

This is called the Pareto distribution in business. For most companies, it looks like this:

80% of revenue comes from 20% of customers.

But sometimes it's more extreme:

90% of revenue from 15% of customers.

Or less:

70% of revenue from 25% of customers.

Here's why this question matters:

**First, it tells you how concentrated your business is.**

If 90% of revenue comes from 10% of customers, you're in a high-risk position. Lose a few key accounts and your business shakes. That requires a very different strategy than if 60% of revenue comes from 40% of customers (more distributed, lower risk).

**Second, it tells you where to invest your energy.**

If 80% of revenue comes from 20% of customers, then your retention budget should be heavily skewed toward those 20%. Your customer service team should prioritize them. Your product development should consider their needs.

But most companies distribute resources equally.

**Third, it reveals if you have a "bottom tier problem."**

If the bottom 40% of customers generate less than 5% of revenue, are they worth keeping? Are you spending more to service them than they generate? Would it be better to let them go naturally and redirect that service cost to high-value customers?

One brand asked this question and realized their bottom 30% of customers (by revenue) were actually negative profit after service and support costs. They gradually stopped pursuing that tier of customer and reallocated the service team to high-value accounts.

Results: Same total service cost, but concentrated on customers where the ROI was positive. Profit margin went up 8%.

**Here's the practical exercise:**

1. Pull your customer database (last 12 months)
2. Sort by total revenue generated per customer (highest to lowest)
3. Calculate cumulative revenue percentage as you go down the list
4. Find the inflection point: "At what percentage of customers do I reach 80% of revenue?"

Most businesses find this takes 15-25% of their customer base.

Then ask: "Am I allocating my resources to match this distribution?"

Usually the answer is no.

**Closing Punch Line:**
You can't optimize what you don't measure. And most businesses don't measure this.

**CTA:**

## POST 9: How One Address Normalization Project Saved a Distributor 15% in Delivery Costs

**Hook Line:**
A distributor's delivery costs were 8% higher than their nearest competitor. It wasn't a logistics problem. It was an address problem.

**Body:**
Here's the story:

A distributor in São Paulo, Brazil was doing about $6M in annual revenue. Their gross margin was solid at 28%. Their logistics were reasonable. But something was wrong.

Their delivery costs per order were 12% of order value. Competitors were at 8-9%. That 3-4% difference was eating into net margin significantly.

The initial assumption was logistics inefficiency. Maybe they needed better routing. Maybe their trucks were underutilized.

But when we dug into the data, we found the real problem: bad address data.

When I say bad, I mean:

- Inconsistent formatting (Av. vs Avenue vs Avenida)
- Missing unit numbers (Apartment 201 vs left blank)
- Non-standardized street names (Rua vs R. vs Street)
- Spelling variations and abbreviations
- GPS coordinates that didn't match the address

Roughly 14% of their 18,000 delivery addresses in the system were either unmappable or required manual intervention before the truck driver could find the location.

What that meant in practice:

Driver shows up to address. GPS doesn't match the written address. Driver has to call customer, ask for clarification, circle the block. This happens 2,500 times per year (14% of 18,000).

Each failed attempt costs: extra fuel, driver time, phone call. Roughly $3 per address failure.

2,500 × $3 = $7,500 per year in direct cost.

But that doesn't account for delivery delays. When drivers are lost, they're behind on their route. They miss other deliveries. Customers get deliveries a day late. Complaints go up. Returns go up. Churn goes up.

The real cost was probably 2-3x higher than the direct costs.

Here's what we did:

1. Took the 18,000 addresses
2. Ran them through an address standardization algorithm
3. Geocoded all of them to precise GPS coordinates
4. Cross-referenced with customer contact info to flag mismatches
5. Delivered a clean, standardized address file

It took about two weeks of work. Cost: $2,400.

Then the distributor had to update their system with the clean data. Cost: one Friday afternoon of IT time.

Results in the following six months:

- First-time delivery success rate went from 86% to 96%
- Failed deliveries dropped 63%
- Average delivery cost per order dropped from 12% to 10.2%
- On $6M in revenue, that's 1.8% in savings = $108,000
- Customer complaints about late deliveries dropped 47%

Net margin improved from 28% to 29.8%.

Cost: $2,400

Savings: $108,000 in first six months, recurring

ROI: 4,500% in year one.

The lesson: sometimes the biggest operational inefficiencies aren't about process or logistics. They're about data quality.

**Closing Punch Line:**
Clean data compounds over time. Dirty data costs you continuously.
